05 / The Water

We will restore the aquifers.

Yes, the ancient aquifers under the Sahara. The ones that took 20,000 years to fill. We're going to top them up. Here's the plan, which we workshopped extensively:

Refill,
baby,
refill.
The last century said "drill, baby, drill" and pumped the ground dry. We took the same rigs, the same crews, the same chant — and reversed exactly one word. Meltwater goes down. The aquifer comes back.

Plan A: Cure the salty sea. Take the ocean, remove the salt, pour it in. (Desalination at septillion-grain scale. Too obvious. Everyone's doing it.)

Plan B: Fresh. Fresh, fresh, fresh melting glaciers will replenish the aquifers. That's the plan.
  1. The glaciers are already melting. This is the part of the plan that is going extremely well, ahead of schedule, with zero effort on our part.
  2. Melting glaciers are fresh water. Pre-desalinated by nature. The salt never got in. Nature's filtration, zero moving parts.
  3. Freshwater goes into the aquifers. Logistics team is confident. The water is in the north; the aquifers are underneath. Down is a direction water famously loves.
  4. Aquifers: restored. The Sahara was green 6,000 years ago. We're simply rolling back to a previous version. Ctrl+Z for a continent.

Critics ask: "Isn't relying on melting glaciers a bad sign?" We prefer to think of the cryosphere as a scheduled delivery. The planet is defrosting the fridge, and we brought a bucket.

The oil & gas industry has a new job.

Here is the beautiful part. There already exists an industry with a century of experience in pumping enormous quantities of liquid across deserts, oceans, and continents. Wrong liquid. Wrong direction. Otherwise flawless. We are simply rehiring the entire sector and flipping the arrows.

🛢️

Pipelines, Reversed

Millions of kilometres of pipeline already cross the planet, expertly welded by people who were told it was for something else. Meltwater in, aquifer out. The infrastructure doesn't care what flows through it. Neither, historically, did the industry.

🚢

A supertanker holds 2 million barrels. It genuinely does not mind whether that's crude or fresh glacier melt — one of them even rinses the tank on the way. Greenland to the Gate, round trips, forever. Or at least until the ice runs out, which — see step 4.

The chant stays; the direction flips. Crews keep their rigs, their hard hats, and their enormous salaries — the only change order is to put the liquid back. Injection wells straight into the aquifer. Same skill, opposite sin.

06 / The Calendar

13 months. 28 days each. No kidding. Serious business.

Sahara Elongated City does not observe your calendar. We operate a parallel system: 13 months of exactly 28 days, aligned with the Moon, which has been keeping perfect time for 4.5 billion years without a software update.

Every month is identical. Every month starts on Sunday. The 1st is always a Sunday, the 15th is always a Sunday, payday is always the 28th, and rent is always your problem. If you know today's date, you know the weekday — forever. Meetings can no longer be "lost track of." We apologise for what this does to your excuses.

13 × 28 = 364 days every month identical. every quarter identical. accountants weep with joy
+1 Year Day day 365 belongs to no month, no week, no obligations. mandatory city-wide holiday in the dunes
13th month: "Sol" inserted between June and July. named after the sun, which we have plenty of
13 Friday the 13ths per year every month has one. we consider fear of this a legacy emotion

Sceptics call this madness. Historians call it the International Fixed Calendar — a real proposal from 1902, and Kodak genuinely ran its entire company on it until 1989. If it's good enough for the people who invented the photograph of your grandparents, it's good enough for a city-state made of sand.

The Machine prints one home per day. Under our calendar, that is exactly 28 homes per month, every month, no exceptions. For the first time in history, a construction schedule and reality are the same document. On Year Day, The Machine prints nothing, and we all watch it rest.

09 / The Lagoons

The excess sand goes to sea. On purpose. To build life.

The Machine prints homes. The foundries print spaceships. But a desert this large produces more sand than even we can launch — so the surplus gets a second career: we build lagoons with it, out in international waters, and we plant them.

Barged offshore and shaped into sheltered atolls, the excess sand becomes the foundation for brand-new coastal ecosystems — warm, shallow lagoons seeded with seagrass, coral nurseries, and fish. Land that was nowhere becomes somewhere. It's terraforming, except the planet we're forming is this one.

Sand → Atoll

Surplus Saharan sand, dredged and placed into engineered shallows in the open ocean. A ring of new seabed in water that was too deep for anything to live. Foundations first — the ocean supplies the rest.

Mangrove Technology

We plant the edges with mangroves — nature's seawall. Their roots trap sediment, break waves, and grow the shoreline outward instead of losing it. The more storms hit, the more land they build. Anti-fragile coastline, from a tree.

New Ecosystems

Seagrass meadows, coral nurseries, nesting birds, spawning fish. A living lagoon within a decade. We don't own the fish. We don't tax the fish. The fish are the one part of this project with genuinely no strings attached.

Here's the honest part, and we'll say it plainly: most of this section is real science. Mangroves really are the best coastal carbon-and-erosion technology on Earth, and moving material to build habitat really works. Take away the gate, the spaceships and the Ministry of Vibes, and what's left underneath is a genuinely good idea. 10 / The Ask
Real · Y Combinator asked for this

Y Combinator literally requested this startup.

We didn't invent "flood the Sahara" for a joke. Y Combinator — the startup accelerator behind Airbnb, Stripe, and Dropbox — published a Request for Startups called Desert Flooding, as part of their carbon-removal program. Contributors include Sam Altman and ocean-carbon scientist Greg Rau. It is real. You can look it up. We'll wait.

The pitch, roughly in YC's own terms:

Flood the desert to create millions of 1 km² oases — shallow ~2 m reservoirs — and seed them with phytoplankton that pull carbon out of the air, while creating newly habitable land. To sequester ~41 gigatons of CO₂ a year — slightly more than humanity emits — you'd flood roughly half the Sahara: about 9 trillion m³ of water.

We do all of the above.

Most answers to that RFS would pick one mechanism. The Elongated City's entire premise is that you don't choose — you stack every source of water and every use of it into one continent-scale system. Here's the full checklist, and every item on it is a real, documented technique:

✓ Recharge the aquifers — managed aquifer recharge, "refill baby refill." → The Water
✓ Flood the Qattara Depression — sub-sea-level basin, seawater hydro. → Qattara subpage
✓ Refill Lake Chad — the Transaqua interbasin transfer for 30M people. → Lake Chad subpage
✓ Build coastal lagoons & mangroves — blue carbon, erosion control. → The Lagoons
✓ The phytoplankton oases — YC's own mechanism: 1 km² reservoirs sequestering carbon. → this section
✓ Desert solar — powering every pump, tunnel, and foundry above. → The Sand

Sold by the linear kilometre. Measured in the square kilometre. YC wanted millions of 1 km² oases — the Elongated City is the parcel map that delivers them, wrapped in a gate, a calendar, a language, and a Ministry of Vibes so that anyone actually reads the RFS.
