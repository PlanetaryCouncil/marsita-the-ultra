01 / The People
Real, and staggering

Thirty million people. One shrinking lake.

Around 30 million people across Chad, Niger, Nigeria, and Cameroon depend on the Lake Chad basin for fishing, farming, and herding — some estimates for the wider basin run far higher. It is one of the most important water bodies in Africa, and it is nearly empty.

Read that number again, because it is the whole reason this page exists. Not tourists. Not residents-in-waiting. Thirty million people whose food and water sit on top of a lake that has lost most of itself inside a single lifetime. That is not a brochure statistic. That is an emergency wearing a statistic's clothes.

02 / The Shrink
Real hydrology

How a great lake vanished in sixty years

In the 1960s Lake Chad covered around 25,000 km². By the 2000s it had collapsed to as little as ~1,350 km² — a loss of roughly nine-tenths. It's shallow, it's endorheic (no outlet to the sea), and that makes it terrifyingly sensitive.

Three forces drained it at once: a drier, hotter climate; booming demand for irrigation as the basin's population grew; and the plain shallowness of the lake, which was never more than a few metres deep. A small drop in rainfall or a big rise in withdrawal, and the map redraws itself. The honest picture: the lake fluctuates and the southern pool persists, but the long trend has been brutal.

🌡️

Less Rain

The Sahel dried through the 1970s–80s. Inflow from the Chari River — which supplies most of the lake — fell with it.

🚜

More Straws

More people, more irrigation, more water pulled out upstream. Demand climbed exactly as supply fell. The lake paid the difference.

📏

Too Shallow To Forgive

A lake a few metres deep has no buffer. Endorheic and flat, it turns small deficits into vast retreating shorelines.

03 / The Refill
Real megaproject · genuinely proposed

Transaqua: borrow water from the Congo

Here's the "insane, but real" part. There is a genuine, decades-old plan to refill Lake Chad by moving water across the continent from the mighty Congo basin — the second-largest river system on Earth, which pours most of its flow uselessly into the Atlantic. It's called Transaqua.

Proposed by the Italian engineering firm Bonifica in the 1980s and seriously revived at an international conference in Abuja in 2018, the scheme would build a long canal from tributaries of the Congo, channel a small fraction of their flow northward into the Chari River, and let it flow down into Lake Chad — refilling it, generating hydropower along the way, and supporting irrigation and transport for the countries it crosses.

The Transaqua idea, in one line

Congo basin → ~2,400 km canal → Chari River → Lake Chad → refilled

A fraction of the Congo's flow, borrowed and sent north. Hydropower and irrigation the whole way. Real proposal. Real feasibility studies. Not yet a real canal.

💧

Water To Spare

The Congo discharges an ocean's worth of freshwater into the Atlantic. Transaqua asks for a single-digit percentage of it. The donor barely notices; the recipient comes back to life.

⚡

Power En Route

A canal with that much fall can run hydro stations along its length — the project could pay part of its own way in electricity for the region it crosses.

🤝

Shared By Design

It would cross and benefit a string of nations — irrigation, navigation, power. That's also the hard part: a project this big needs unprecedented cross-border trust. That, not the digging, is the real obstacle.

We would not pretend this is easy, cheap, or without serious environmental questions of its own — interbasin transfers can do real ecological harm and deserve real scrutiny. But it is a genuine idea that serious people keep returning to, precisely because the alternative is watching thirty million livelihoods dry out.

04 / Why It Matters
No punchline here either

A dry lake is a security crisis.

When a lake that feeds millions retreats, the people don't vanish with it — they crowd onto the last wet ground, they migrate, they lose their income, and desperation becomes a resource that armed groups recruit from. The shrinking of Lake Chad is tangled up with displacement, hunger, and years of violent instability across the region.

So "refill Lake Chad" isn't landscape gardening. It's food for farmers, water for herders, fish for fishers, and a reason for young people to stay and build instead of flee or fight. Restore the lake and you're not just moving water — you're removing the vacuum that catastrophe grows in.

  1. Support what already exists. The Lake Chad Basin Commission and the four riparian states are real institutions already working on this. Any serious effort strengthens them, it doesn't bypass them.
  2. Small, local wins first. Efficient irrigation, restored wetlands, and better water management help now — years before any continental canal could.
  3. Study the big transfer honestly. Transaqua deserves rigorous, independent environmental and hydrological review — not hype, and not dismissal.
  4. Keep the people at the centre. Thirty million of them. They are the project. The engineering is just how we keep the promise.
