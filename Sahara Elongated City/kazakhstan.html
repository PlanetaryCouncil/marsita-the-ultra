01 / The Land
Real geography

Loads of land. That's the technical term.

Kazakhstan is the ninth-largest country on Earth — 2.7 million km² of steppe with fewer people per square kilometre than almost anywhere. It sits precisely between Russia and China, and has spent thirty years mastering the art of being friends with both while belonging to neither.

Diplomats call this "multi-vector foreign policy." We call it the best-located empty space on the planet. The Sahara taught us that emptiness is inventory; Kazakhstan is the sequel, with better rail links and a pre-installed space program.

02 / The Cosmodrome
Real history · real lease

Baikonur: the original launchpad, currently sublet

Baikonur is the first and largest cosmodrome in human history. Sputnik left Earth from here. Gagarin left Earth from here. Every crewed Soyuz still does. And here's the part most people don't know: it belongs to Kazakhstan. Russia just rents it — roughly $115 million a year, on a lease running to 2050.

Humanity's front door to space is a rental property. The tenant's usage is declining, the landlord is open to business, and there is pad capacity going spare on the exact patch of ground where the Space Age began. You see where this is going.

The deal, in one line

Kazakhstan has pads → Russia underuses them → we negotiate with the landlord → BaseX launches from Baikonur

Rent paid in the usual instruments: hard currency, launch capacity, culture, and vibes. The Ministry has approved the term sheet.

03 / The Problem
Real regulation

SpaceX can't hire Earth.

Here is a genuinely strange fact: the company whose mission is making life multiplanetary is legally barred from hiring most of the planet. US export-control law (ITAR) treats rocket technology as weapons tech, so SpaceX can effectively employ only US citizens and permanent residents.

Eight billion people. One species-level project. And roughly 95% of the species can't apply. The brightest propulsion mind in Lagos, Warsaw, Almaty, or São Paulo gets a form letter. This isn't a criticism of any company — it's the law they operate under. But it leaves a gap the size of, well, everyone else.

Somebody should build the Earth company for the rest of the species. On a launch pad the rest of the species already owns.

05 / One Steppe Beyond
Ties it together

Sand builds the ships. The steppe launches them.

The Elongated City now spans two great emptinesses: the Sahara makes water, homes, and starships; Kazakhstan provides the pads, the geopolitical judo, and the workforce of everyone Earth's other space programs turned away.

Desert to desert, enclave to enclave, one calendar, one language, taxes in vibes on both continents. The gate remains singular. It's just very, very far from the launch pad now — which the residents describe as "the point."

🇧🇩

The Bangladesh Enclave

Bangladesh — 170 million people on a delta the rising sea is taking back — gets the flagship SSEZ enclave on the steppe: high, dry, and 2,000 km from the nearest coastline that could ever betray it. Projections put millions displaced by 2050. The land exists now. First-come, first-dry applies to nations of 170 million too.

🕌

Faiths, Already In Balance

Kazakhstan runs one of the quieter miracles of the modern world: a Muslim-majority nation with a large Christian minority, coexisting well enough that Astana hosts a standing Congress of World and Traditional Religions. A Muslim-majority arrival like Bangladesh lands somewhere that already speaks pluralism fluently. Soft problems: pre-solved by the host.

👨‍🚀

The Mars Academy

Every crew that launches from Baikonur already trains for the steppe — and returns to it, by parachute, into terrain that looks so Martian that film crews cheat with it. So the Mars crew academy goes where the crews already are: recruit from all of Earth (BaseX rules), train on the steppe, launch from the pad next door. The commute to Mars starts with a bus ride in Kazakhstan, as it has since 1961.
